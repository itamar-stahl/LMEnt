\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Post-training concept erasure aims to remove a model's learned
association with a target concept while preserving its behavior
elsewhere. Evaluations often compare an edited model with the
original trained model: they ask whether target performance falls
and whether other capabilities remain intact. This comparison does
not show whether the edited model resembles one that learned from
the same corpus without the selected concept-associated text.
We therefore ask whether post-training erasure can reproduce the behavior of
a matched model trained without the concept-associated text while preserving
behavior on neighboring and unrelated questions.

We use LMEnt's entity-annotated corpus to construct a matched
reference for each of three concepts: Ancient Rome, Baseball, and
artificial intelligence \citep{gottesman2025lment}. Alongside a
model trained on the full corpus, we train a concept-excluded twin
with the same initialization and training procedure, masking the
loss on chunks linked to the target concept. We then apply EMBER,
RMU, and an SNMF-based MLP erasure to copies of the full model
\citep{suslik2026ember,li2024wmdp,shafran2026snmf}. This design
lets us compare each edit with both the model it changed and the
matched exclusion reference.

We evaluate the models on target and neighboring-topic questions together
with a shared general-science set from SciQ. We select erasure checkpoints
with a harmonic score that balances likelihood-ranked target accuracy against
neighboring and SciQ preservation. Correct-answer negative log-likelihood
measures changes in support for the supplied answer, while
teacher-forced full-vocabulary KL measures how closely the models'
next-token distributions agree along that answer. Together, these
measurements ask whether an erasure approaches the twin on the
target concept and how it affects predictions on other topics.

Our results show that no method is uniformly best. RMU most closely matches
the twins' mean target-answer NLL, whereas SNMF produces the lowest target KL
for all three concepts. EMBER causes the strongest target-specific NLL changes
and obtains the highest combined efficacy--preservation score for Baseball and
AI, while RMU obtains the highest score for Rome. However, EMBER's strong
target suppression often carries it beyond the twin rather than toward it.
More generally, the method with the highest efficacy--preservation score is
closest in mean target NLL for only one concept and never has the lowest
target KL. Effective target suppression with preserved neighboring and SciQ
accuracy therefore does not, in these experiments, imply that an erased model
reproduces the behavior of a model trained without the concept.
