\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Language models may retain knowledge that their developers later wish to
remove, for example because it is private, copyrighted, obsolete, or unsafe.
Post-hoc concept erasure attempts to remove such knowledge from model
parameters while preserving unrelated capabilities. A successful method should
do more than change answers to a familiar prompt: its effect should persist
across task formats and should not merely hide accessible knowledge.

Most evaluations compare an erased model only with its original version. This
establishes that an intervention changed measured behavior, but not that the
result resembles genuine absence of learning. An edit can be too weak, too
strong, or damaging in a qualitatively different way while still appearing to
remove knowledge of the target. Retraining after removing concept-associated
data supplies a more natural reference, but the expense of training and the
difficulty of identifying concept-related data have usually made this baseline
unavailable.

We use LMEnt's entity-annotated corpus and open training pipeline
\citep{gottesman2025lment} to construct matched OLMo-2 1B models
\citep{walsh2025olmo2}. A shared control model is trained on the complete corpus,
while three matched models receive no loss on training text segments, or
\emph{chunks}, identified with Ancient Rome, Baseball, or artificial
intelligence (AI). Entity filtering cannot
guarantee removal of every indirect mention, so we call these models
\emph{concept-excluded}; they are our
operational never-trained references.

Our primary method is EMBER \citep{suslik2026ember}, which removes
concept-associated sparse features from selected token embeddings.
Representation Misdirection for Unlearning (RMU) \citep{li2024wmdp} and
semi-nonnegative matrix factorization (SNMF)
\citep{shafran2026snmf} provide comparisons that edit transformer multilayer
perceptron (MLP) blocks. We ask:

\begin{quote}
\small
How closely do post-hoc erasure methods reproduce a matched model trained
without learning signal from concept-associated text?
\end{quote}

The EMBER results depend strongly on concept and measurement. EMBER increases
Rome text loss much more than exclusion does and Baseball text loss less than
exclusion does, while approaching the AI reference on held-out questions. A
Rome strength sweep
shows that matching the excluded model's mean loss change does not reproduce
the distribution across passages. The Baseball edit also has almost no
directional alignment with the parameter change from control to exclusion.

Our contributions are threefold:

\begin{itemize}
  \item We construct controlled concept-excluded references for three concepts
  while holding initialization, batch order, optimizer recipe, and duration
  fixed.
  \item We compare erasure with those references using behavioral, text-loss,
  parameter-space, and unrelated-data measurements where available.
  \item We show that proximity depends on concept, edit strength, and the
  measurement level; lexical analysis explains a major mismatch for EMBER.
\end{itemize}
